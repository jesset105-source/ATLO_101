Procedure Development & Control

A test procedure is the most important document an ATLO engineer works with. It tells you exactly what to do, in what order, with what expected results -- and it is the record of what actually happened to the spacecraft.

Why Procedures Matter

In ATLO, there is no free-form testing. Every operation on the spacecraft -- every command sent, every connector mated, every power rail enabled -- is done following a documented, approved procedure. This is not bureaucracy. It is mission assurance.

The stakes are high. A procedure step executed out of sequence can damage hardware. A command sent to the wrong address can put a subsystem in an unexpected state. An undocumented action during a test cannot be traced if something goes wrong afterward.

When you deviate from a procedure -- even for good reason -- you document it. When a procedure step produces an unexpected result, you stop and document it before continuing. The procedure is the record of what actually happened to the spacecraft.

Procedure Validation on Flatsat

Procedures are authored before they are executed on the flight vehicle. They are validated on flatsat first -- run against engineering model hardware to verify that the steps are correct, the expected values are accurate, and the sequence is complete.

This validation process is where most procedure problems are caught: a step that references the wrong telemetry point, an expected value that doesn't match what the hardware actually produces, a sequence missing a prerequisite. If you are authoring a procedure, your job isn't done until it has been successfully executed on flatsat.

Version Control and the Approval Process

Procedures are version-controlled documents. Every change -- no matter how small -- requires a new revision. The revision history tracks who changed what, when, and why.

Before a procedure can be used on the flight vehicle, it must go through a review and approval process -- typically the ATLO lead, the systems engineer, the affected subsystem engineer, and QA. The approval signatures indicate that qualified people have reviewed the steps and expected results and found them correct.

Using an unapproved procedure on the flight vehicle is a configuration control violation. Using an out-of-date revision -- even if the changes are minor -- is the same. Procedure control is not flexible.

Written Exercise — Write a Procedure Step

Draft your answer, then reveal a model answer to self-check.
System configuration: Propellant valve PV-7 is currently CLOSED. You need to command it OPEN and verify it worked. Ground command: PROP_PV7_OPEN_CMD. Telemetry point: PROP_VALVE_7_STATE (expected values: OPEN or CLOSED). Write one complete procedure step -- Action, Expected Result, and Pass/Fail Criterion -- for this operation.
MODEL ANSWER Action: Send command PROP_PV7_OPEN_CMD to open propellant valve PV-7.
Expected Result: Verify telemetry point PROP_VALVE_7_STATE transitions from CLOSED to OPEN within 3 seconds of command execution.
Pass/Fail Criterion: PASS if PROP_VALVE_7_STATE = OPEN within 3 seconds of command send; FAIL if it remains CLOSED or transitions to any other state.
  • Is your action specific enough that another engineer could execute it without asking a question?
  • Does your expected result name the exact telemetry point and the value it should reach?
  • Is your pass/fail condition a yes/no test, with no room for interpretation?
