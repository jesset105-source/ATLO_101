Electrical Fundamentals: Schematics & Circuit Basics

Before you ever touch a multimeter to a spacecraft connector, you have to know two things cold: what the circuit is supposed to do, and what number your meter should show if it's doing it. That starts with reading a schematic.

Why This Lesson Comes First in Execution

Every subsystem you will integrate in this module — heaters, thermistors, valves, reaction wheels, avionics boxes — is described by a set of engineering drawings before it is ever hardware in your hands. Reading those drawings correctly, and calculating what a healthy circuit should measure before you measure it, is what separates a real verification from guessing.

This is not course-invented theory. The diagram types and measurement discipline in this lesson come directly from the Navy Electricity and Electronics Training Series (NEETS) — the same public-domain training curriculum used to teach electricians and technicians these fundamentals for decades. Module 4, "Schematic Reading," and Module 3, "Circuit Protection, Control, and Measuring Devices," are cited throughout.

Schematic vs. Wiring Diagram — Two Views of the Same Circuit

A schematic diagram shows the electrical relationship between components — what is connected to what, and through what — using standard symbols. It does not show you where those components physically are, or which pin on a real connector you are looking at.

A wiring diagram (or terminal diagram, for a single connector) shows the physical reality: real connectors, real pin numbers, real terminal boards. NEETS Module 4 states this directly: the schematic tells you where the trouble electrically should be; the wiring diagram tells you where to actually put your meter leads to check it.

Below is both views of the same fictional connector, J1, which you will use for the CSTM exercise later in this lesson. Compare them side by side.

Fig. 2.1-A — Schematic Diagram

The electrical relationship only. Resistor symbol for the heater, thermistor symbol for the temperature sensor — same convention NEETS Module 4 uses for its water-temperature element (Fig. 3-10).
Two isolated circuits live inside this one connector. Nothing connects Row 1 (A–B) to Row 2 (C–D) — that isolation is a design requirement, and it is exactly what a CSTM check verifies.

Fig. 2.1-B — Wiring / Terminal Diagram

The physical mating face — where you actually place your multimeter probes.
Notice the schematic never told you A and B were physically adjacent, diagonal, or anywhere in particular. You need the terminal diagram for that. This is why an ATLO engineer reviews both drawings before ever picking up a meter.

Ohm's Law and the Ohmmeter

Ohm's Law — V = I × R — is the relationship every resistance calculation in this course comes back to (NEETS Module 1, "Introduction to Matter, Energy, and Direct Current"). For a resistive heater with a known power spec, the more useful form is R = V² / P, derived from P = V²/R.

An ohmmeter measures resistance by pushing a small current from its own internal battery through the circuit under test and reading the result — which is exactly why NEETS Module 3 lists this as the first safety rule for using one: the circuit must be de-energized before you connect an ohmmeter in series with it. The meter supplies its own source. If the circuit already has power on it, the two sources fight, and at best you get a meaningless reading — at worst you damage the meter.

This is the same logic behind a CSTM check: it happens before the connector is mated and before the circuit is powered, using nothing but a multimeter and the resistance you calculated ahead of time from the schematic.

KEY POINT

A CSTM check is really just this sequence, every time: read the schematic to know what should be there → calculate the expected value with Ohm's Law → find the physical pins on the wiring/terminal diagram → measure resistance with the circuit unpowered → compare. If the exercise ahead feels procedural, that's because it is — and that's the point.

Exercise — Calculate the Expected Resistance

Draft your calculation, then reveal a model answer to self-check.
Per Fig. 2.1-A, the patch heater on Pins A–B is specified to dissipate 32 W at the nominal spacecraft bus voltage of 28 VDC. Using R = V² / P, calculate the expected resistance between Pins A and B. Then propose a pass/fail tolerance band a CSTM procedure could use (industry-typical practice is ±10%).
MODEL ANSWER

R = V² / P = 28² / 32 = 784 / 32 = 24.5 Ω

With a ±10% tolerance, a CSTM procedure would call out a pass/fail band of approximately 22.05 Ω to 26.95 Ω (commonly rounded to 22–27 Ω). A reading inside that band on Pins A–B is a pass. A reading of 0 Ω (short) or OL/infinite (open) is an automatic hold, regardless of how "close" it might otherwise look.

  • Did you square the voltage, not just multiply it by itself once incorrectly?
  • Does your tolerance band bracket your calculated value symmetrically?

Scenario — Reading the CSTM Results

You just ran the CSTM check on connector J1 before it was ever mated to the flight harness. Read the results, then choose what you do next.
Pins A–B (heater): your meter reads 24.6 Ω — inside the 22–27 Ω band you calculated. Pins C–D (thermistor): you expected roughly 10 kΩ at room temperature, but your meter reads OL (open circuit, infinite resistance). What do you do?
