The Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

Before you can build a schedule, you need to know what work exists. That's what the Work Breakdown Structure is for. A WBS breaks the entire ATLO campaign into discrete, manageable tasks -- starting at the highest level (campaign phases) and decomposing down to individual activities. The WBS gives every task a home in the hierarchy and ensures nothing is missed.

Building a WBS requires knowing the campaign -- what hardware is coming, what tests need to be run, what reviews are required, what environmental testing is planned. An experienced ATLO engineer contributes to the WBS by identifying the specific tasks and durations for the operations they own.

Task Dependencies

Once you have a list of tasks, you need to understand which ones depend on each other. Some tasks cannot start until other tasks are complete; some can run in parallel. Getting the dependencies right is what makes a schedule realistic. Examples from an ATLO campaign:

  • You cannot run an end-to-end RF functional test until the RF subsystem is installed and powered up
  • You cannot power up the flight vehicle electrically until the ISTM checks confirm the harness is correct
  • You cannot begin TVAC until all open DRs that could affect the test are dispositioned
  • You cannot ship the vehicle until the Pre-Shipment Review is complete and all closure criteria are met

Task dependencies create a chain. If one task slips, everything downstream of it potentially slips.

Launch Window Constraints

Spacecraft launches are not flexible the way most project milestones are. A launch window is determined by orbital mechanics -- the geometry of the Earth, the target orbit, and the launch vehicle's capabilities. Miss the window, and the next one may be days, weeks, or months away.

This means the launch date is often the most fixed point in the entire program. Everything in the campaign schedule is built backward from that date. ATLO is the last major phase before launch, which means ATLO schedule slips directly threaten the launch window -- schedule discipline here is not bureaucratic box-checking.

NPR 7120.5 Awareness

The campaign schedule is not just an internal planning tool -- it is what NPR 7120.5, NASA's Space Flight Program and Project Management Requirements, uses to govern when major program reviews happen. Launch Readiness Reviews and Pre-Shipment Reviews are scheduled milestones, not afterthoughts -- they exist as gates in the same schedule you are learning to build. When you place a review on your schedule, you are working within the same review structure NPR 7120.5 defines for real NASA programs.

Cost Consequences of Schedule Slip

Every task in the ATLO schedule has a cost associated with it -- labor, facility time, GSE rental, personnel travel. When tasks take longer than planned, those costs increase. The Earned Value Management (EVM) system tracks the relationship between schedule performance and cost performance, and the Estimate at Completion (EAC) changes every time a test runs long, a DR is opened, or scope is added.

This is why test preparation matters. An ATLO engineer who understands the procedure, has GSE configured correctly, and has expected values documented before the test starts is far less likely to introduce delays than one figuring things out during execution. Preparation is cost avoidance.

What Drives Schedule Slip in ATLO

From real campaign experience, the most common drivers of ATLO schedule slip are: Discrepancy Reports (every DR requires investigation and disposition time, and some require rework), design changes late in the campaign, added scope that surfaces during execution, test duration overruns, and improper planning -- tasks that were underestimated or had missing dependencies. When you can see a schedule risk coming, surface it early. Early visibility gives the program options; late surprises don't.
